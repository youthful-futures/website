When Did Childhood Become a Résumé?

When every hobby, competition and project can become evidence for a future application, what happens to the parts of childhood that are allowed to go nowhere?

4 min read

Ask a child what they do outside school, and the answer increasingly sounds like a professional biography.

Football.

Piano.

Coding.

Debate.

Model United Nations.

Robotics.

Dance.

Olympiads.

Volunteering.

Language classes.

Test preparation.

Internships.

Content creation.

None of these things is bad. Many are wonderful. Children should discover things, make things, play sports, perform, compete and become good at something.

The interesting question is when all of it began to feel consequential.

A hobby used to be allowed to be something you did badly because you enjoyed it. Now it can become evidence.

Evidence that you are disciplined.

Interesting.

Well-rounded.

Talented.

A leader.

Somebody a future school, university or employer should select.

The résumé has moved backwards into childhood.

It would be easy to blame ambitious parents.

I think that misses the more interesting story.

Parents respond to systems.

When access to attractive universities, secure jobs or prestigious opportunities feels scarce, families look for whatever advantage is available.

When grades stop distinguishing applicants because everybody applying has high grades, another layer emerges.

Leadership.

Projects.

Sport.

Service.

Competitions.

Portfolios.

When everybody begins doing those, distinction has to be found somewhere else.

The arms race can be perfectly rational for each individual family while being exhausting for everyone collectively.

There are signs of the pressure across different education systems. In PISA 2022, 47.3 per cent of students across OECD countries said they worried that they were not prepared for life after compulsory education. Another 43.6 per cent worried that they would not have enough money to do what they wanted afterwards.1

The WHO's large Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study provides another window. Across 44 countries and regions in Europe, Central Asia and Canada, school pressure rose between 2018 and 2022. Among fifteen-year-old girls, 63 per cent reported feeling pressured by schoolwork, compared with 54 per cent four years earlier. Among boys the equivalent figure rose from 40 to 43 per cent.2

These figures are not a global verdict on childhood.

Academic pressure differs enormously between countries and within them. Poverty can place a child under a different kind of pressure from elite university competition.

Millions of young people are still fighting simply to stay in education. UNICEF reported 272 million children and young people out of school in 2024, including 130 million of upper-secondary age.3

That contrast is important.

There are children whose futures are constrained because they cannot access enough education, and children whose futures feel constrained by the fear that no amount of education will be enough.

Both are questions of opportunity.

What worries me about the résumé childhood is not that young people work hard.

Difficulty is not inherently harmful, and adults sometimes underestimate how much satisfaction young people get from mastering something difficult.

The problem is when exploration becomes optimisation.

The difference is subtle.

A child learning guitar because something about music grips them may practise harder than a child learning guitar because it will strengthen an application.

Both may eventually become excellent musicians.

But they are learning something different about themselves.

One discovers:

I care about this.

The other risks discovering:

This makes me more valuable.

That distinction matters especially during adolescence, when identity itself is under construction.

We are asking someone who does not yet know who they are to begin producing evidence of who they will become.

Technology intensifies this because comparison no longer stops at the school gate.

A talented sixteen-year-old used to compare themselves mostly with people they could see.

Now they can encounter the best sixteen-year-old pianist, programmer, footballer, debater, entrepreneur and artist on Earth before breakfast.

The exceptional becomes ordinary through exposure.

We should be careful here too.

Seeing what other young people create can be exhilarating. It can expand a child's sense of possibility. A teenager in a small town can discover peers who care about astrophysics, animation or obscure music in a way that would have been almost impossible a generation ago.

The same mechanism that generates comparison can generate belonging.

That is why "reduce pressure" is too simple a prescription.

Some pressure comes from meaningful ambition.

Some comes from financial insecurity.

Some comes from social comparison.

Some comes from parental fear.

Some comes from young people's own desire to become excellent.

Treating all of it as pathology strips them of agency.

I am more interested in a different question.

Does childhood still contain enough activity whose value does not depend on where it leads?

Can you paint without becoming an artist?

Play a sport without making the team?

Learn code without founding something?

Read without improving your vocabulary?

Volunteer without documenting it?

Build something terrible and never upload it?

The strange thing about a résumé is that it gives every experience a destination.

You did this so that someone in the future would conclude something about you.

But some of the most important things young people do may matter precisely because they go nowhere.

They are how you discover what you love, what bores you, what you are bad at, what kind of people you enjoy being around and what you want to spend three hours doing even if nobody rewards you.

A childhood that prepares somebody for everything may accidentally leave too little room for them to discover anything.

And perhaps that is the question worth asking before we add the next class, credential or competition:

If nobody were ever going to see this on an application, would we still want the child to do it?
