Same Age, Different Amounts of Freedom

Age tells us when somebody is supposed to become independent. It tells us much less about the choices they are actually able to make.

4 min read

Age is one of the simplest ways we organise childhood.

At thirteen you can do this.

At sixteen you can do that.

At eighteen you become legally adult.

It creates the impression that freedom arrives roughly on schedule.

It does not.

Two seventeen-year-olds can have radically different amounts of control over their own lives.

One decides when to go out, whom to meet, what to study and what to wear.

Another asks permission for each of those things.

One has a bedroom, laptop, bank account and parents willing to pay for university.

Another is caring for siblings, contributing to household income or travelling hours to school.

One enters adulthood with savings.

Another enters it with family debt.

And in much of the world, gender changes the equation further.

Globally, girls aged 5 to 14 spend an estimated 160 million more hours every day on unpaid care and domestic work than boys of the same age.1 Among 10-to-14-year-olds globally, 16 per cent of girls spend more than 21 hours a week on household chores, compared with 8 per cent of boys. In South Asia and the Middle East and North Africa, girls are three times as likely as boys to cross that threshold.2

That is not merely an inequality in chores.

It is an inequality in time.

Time to study.

Time to play.

Time to make friends.

Time to practise something.

Time to discover what you might be good at.

The divergence continues into work.

Two-thirds of young people globally who are outside employment, education or training are women.3

Even access to the supposedly borderless internet is shaped by the same old structures.

In 2025, 77 per cent of men globally were online compared with 71 per cent of women, a gap amounting to roughly 280 million more male than female internet users. The divide remains largest in lower-income settings.4

The important word here is not inequality.

It is freedom.

We often measure inequality through outcomes.

Who earned more?

Who completed school?

Who became a manager?

Freedom asks an earlier question.

What options were genuinely available?

The distinction matters enormously when talking about young people.

A twenty-year-old who lives with their parents because family life is important to them is in a different situation from somebody who lives there because they cannot afford to leave.

A young woman who chooses not to work outside the home is in a different situation from one prevented from doing so.

A teenager who spends evenings caring for a younger sibling may love doing it and still have fewer free hours than a classmate.

The visible behaviour can be identical.

The freedom behind it is not.

This is why global conversations about "this generation" collapse so quickly.

Even legal adulthood does not bring equal agency.

The World Bank's 2026 Women, Business and the Law study examined 190 economies and concluded that no economy provides women with equal economic opportunities across the legal and institutional framework it measures. Only 4 per cent of women live in economies close to full legal equality.5

Class changes freedom just as dramatically.

A young person with financial support can take an unpaid internship, leave a harmful job, move cities, prepare for an examination for a year, start a business that fails or choose work they find meaningful.

Someone without that support may appear more "practical".

Often, practicality is simply what choice looks like when the margin for error is small.

Geography matters too.

The internet has expanded access to knowledge but has not erased physical infrastructure.

A course can be online.

A laboratory cannot always be.

A remote job can exist, but reliable electricity and broadband may not.

A young person can learn that an opportunity exists without having any realistic route to reach it.

This is why access can create frustration as well as empowerment.

You can now see the lives that are unavailable to you in extraordinary detail.

It is also why I am wary of stories about extraordinarily resilient young people overcoming impossible odds.

Those stories can be genuinely inspiring.

They can also allow adults to admire someone's resilience rather than ask why so much resilience was required.

The goal should not be to produce young people capable of surviving every obstacle we place in front of them.

It should be to remove obstacles that serve no good purpose.

This does not mean eliminating difficulty.

A meaningful life contains difficulty.

Learning something hard contains difficulty.

Relationships contain difficulty.

Building competence requires frustration.

But there is a difference between difficulty that develops a person and constraint that simply reduces their options.

Perhaps this is a better way of thinking about equity among young people.

Not making everyone's life identical.

Not guaranteeing identical outcomes.

But asking whether young people have a reasonable field of possibility from which to construct their lives.

The same age does not mean the same adulthood is available.

Before asking what a young person chose, it may be worth asking:

What were they actually free to choose?
