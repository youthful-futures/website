# Youthful Futures

## Website landing page

### Series title

**Youthful Futures**

### Series standfirst

**What does it mean to grow up when the world around you is changing faster than the institutions meant to prepare you for it?**

### Series introduction

We talk about young people as if they are a single generation. They are not.

A thirteen-year-old negotiating school, parents and a first phone is living a very different life from a twenty-two-year-old trying to enter work or a twenty-nine-year-old trying to decide what stability, partnership and adulthood should look like. Those differences grow even larger across countries, classes, genders and families.

What they do share is an unusual historical position. Information is abundant. Artificial intelligence can perform tasks before a beginner has learned how to do them. Education has expanded while the route from education to secure work has become less predictable. Young people have more ways to connect, more lives to compare themselves with and, in some places, more freedom to choose what adulthood looks like. At the same time, many of the institutions surrounding them still operate on assumptions built for a different world.

**Youthful Futures** is a series about those mismatches. It looks at people roughly between 13 and 30, not to diagnose a generation, but to understand the environments in which young people are becoming themselves.

The question running through the series is simple:

**What futures are young people being asked to prepare for, and have we built the institutions that make those futures possible?**

# 1. 13 to 30 Is Not a Generation

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/13-to-30-is-not-a-generation/`

**Dek:** Before diagnosing Gen Z or Gen Alpha, it may be worth asking whether a generation is even the right unit for understanding young people.

**SEO title:** 13 to 30 Is Not a Generation | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** A teenager and a thirty-year-old are not one demographic. The first Youthful Futures essay asks what young people actually share in a rapidly changing world.

**Tags:** youth, generations, adulthood, future, demography

**Suggested related articles:** [Adult Information, Child Power](/youthful-futures/adult-information-child-power/), [The Broken Handoff](/youthful-futures/the-broken-handoff/), [Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable](/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/)

We have become very comfortable talking about generations. Gen Z believes this. Gen Alpha behaves like that. Millennials ruined something else. The categories are convenient because they turn hundreds of millions of people into something that can fit inside a headline.

They are also a strange way to understand a 13-year-old growing up today.

Consider what sits inside the age range of 13 to 30. At thirteen, somebody may still have their phone taken away by a parent before bed. At seventeen, they may be choosing subjects that supposedly determine the rest of their life. At twenty-one, they may be leaving university, caring for family, working full-time, looking for work or doing all four. At twenty-nine, they may be managing people at work while still wondering whether they can afford a home, whether they want children and what exactly being an adult was supposed to feel like.

Calling all of them a generation tells us something about when they were born. It tells us surprisingly little about the lives they are living.

The differences become much larger when we leave the boundaries of a single country. A teenager growing up in a wealthy neighbourhood of Toronto may have more material circumstances in common with a teenager in Singapore than with another teenager several hundred kilometres away. A young woman and young man born on the same day may inherit radically different amounts of freedom. A twenty-four-year-old in a country with a shrinking workforce may enter a labour market desperate for workers; another in a country with a rapidly expanding youth population may be one of thousands competing for each formal job.

This divergence will matter more. UNICEF estimates that the world's child population will remain roughly 2.3 billion into the 2050s, but where those children live will change considerably. South Asia will remain home to one of the world's largest child populations, while Eastern and Southern Africa and West and Central Africa will become increasingly important. At the same time, children will make up a smaller share of many ageing societies. Three in five children are projected to live in cities by the 2050s.[^01-unicef]

The experience of being young is not converging toward one universal childhood. In several important ways, it may be diverging.

Technology complicates this further. Young people are the most connected age group humanity has ever produced. In 2025, 82 per cent of people aged 15 to 24 used the internet globally, compared with 72 per cent of the rest of the population.[^01-itu]

But even that apparently global experience conceals enormous inequality. Youth internet use was estimated at 99 per cent in high-income countries and 38 per cent in low-income countries in the same ITU dataset.[^01-itu] A supposedly shared digital generation does not share the same digital world.

So perhaps generation is not the most useful unit of analysis.

What seems more interesting is **transition**.

Between roughly 13 and 30, human beings pass through an unusual concentration of transitions. They move from dependence toward autonomy. From education toward work. From families they inherit toward relationships and perhaps families they choose. From identities largely organised for them toward identities they construct for themselves.

They acquire political beliefs, sexual identities, professional ambitions, financial responsibilities and ideas about what a good life looks like.

And the institutions surrounding these transitions are changing at different speeds.

A teenager can access more information than the largest libraries available to previous generations, but still attend a school organised around information scarcity.

A university graduate can use artificial intelligence capable of performing professional tasks that previous generations spent years learning, then discover that employers still want experience for an entry-level position.

A young adult can encounter thousands of potential romantic partners through a screen while finding stable relationships difficult to build.

Someone can technically work from almost anywhere while being unable to afford a home anywhere near the economic centre where the work exists.

This is what interests me about young people now. Not whether they are softer, smarter, more anxious, more entitled or more progressive than the people who came before them.

The more useful question is what happens when **the environment in which people are expected to grow up changes faster than our ideas of what growing up is supposed to look like**.

There are real reasons for concern. Globally, one in seven 10-to-19-year-olds experiences a mental disorder. Suicide is the third leading cause of death among people aged 15 to 29.[^01-who] One in five young people globally was outside employment, education or training in 2023.[^01-ilo]

But beginning with crisis would be a mistake.

In one of the largest recent surveys of young adults, UNFPA heard from more than 108,000 internet-connected people aged 18 to 39 across 73 countries. Two-thirds said they felt somewhat or very positive about the future despite worries about conflict, economic insecurity and inequality. More than two-thirds wanted to marry or live with a partner. The survey is not nationally representative, but its geographical breadth is a useful warning against assuming that young adulthood is simply collapsing into despair.[^01-unfpa]

Young people are not merely victims of technological and economic change either. They are often its earliest experimenters. They build communities in places adults barely understand, learn skills through systems schools did not create, invent new kinds of work and routinely use tools that institutions are still holding committees to discuss.

The point of **Youthful Futures** is therefore not to diagnose a generation.

It is to look closely at the environments in which young people are becoming themselves.

Sometimes that will mean asking uncomfortable questions about schools, employers, technology companies, cities and families. Sometimes it will require asking uncomfortable questions about young people themselves. Most often, I suspect, it will mean discovering that the familiar argument between "young people need to change" and "society needs to change" is less useful than we think.

The people who are thirteen today will be thirty in 2043.

Instead of asking what generation they belong to, perhaps we should be asking a harder question.

**What kind of adulthood are we preparing them for?**

# 2. Adult Information, Child Power

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/adult-information-child-power/`

**Dek:** Young people can know almost anything adults know long before they have adult control over their own lives. What does that do to growing up?

**SEO title:** Adult Information, Child Power | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Young people have unprecedented access to information, but information and agency are not the same thing. What happens when knowledge arrives before power?

**Tags:** youth, internet, AI, digital literacy, adolescence

**Suggested related articles:** [Personalised Everywhere, Standardised at School](/youthful-futures/personalised-everywhere-standardised-at-school/), [AI Arrived Before Apprenticeship](/youthful-futures/ai-arrived-before-apprenticeship/)

There is something historically unusual about being thirteen today.

A thirteen-year-old can ask an artificial intelligence system what depression feels like, learn how nuclear weapons work, watch footage from a war thousands of kilometres away, understand what their parents' salary means, learn about sexuality, discover arguments against their family's religion, follow influencers explaining investment strategies and receive step-by-step instructions for building a computer program.

Then somebody can tell them it is time to put the phone away and go to bed.

For most of history, information and authority were entangled. Adults knew things children did not. Teachers had books students did not own. Professionals had access to specialised knowledge that outsiders could not easily reach. Parents could delay conversations because the information itself could often be delayed.

That arrangement has weakened quickly.

Globally, 82 per cent of people aged 15 to 24 are now online. In high-income countries youth internet use is essentially universal, while young people are still substantially more likely than older people to be connected in low-income countries.[^02-itu]

Artificial intelligence pushes this further because access to information is becoming access to explanation.

Search engines gave young people the world's documents. Generative AI increasingly offers to interpret those documents, answer follow-up questions and tailor explanations to the person asking.

In the United States, where good recent data exists, 64 per cent of teenagers aged 13 to 17 told Pew in 2025 that they had used an AI chatbot. About three in ten said they used one daily. ChatGPT alone was used by 59 per cent.[^02-pew]

Those figures should not be carelessly projected onto the world, but they show how quickly a new kind of relationship with information can become normal.

We tend to discuss this as a technology problem.

Are children getting misinformation?

Are they cheating?

How much screen time is healthy?

Should schools ban phones?

Those questions matter.

But a deeper change hides underneath them.

**Young people are receiving adult-scale access to information without adult-scale control over their lives.**

Knowing and being able to act are two different forms of power.

A fifteen-year-old may know that their school is teaching something badly but have little choice over attending. They may understand that a family relationship is unhealthy while being financially dependent on that family. They may know more about a political conflict than many adults but have no vote. They may know precisely which career they want but live somewhere where that pathway barely exists.

Information can therefore liberate and frustrate at the same time.

It can also create a strange inversion of expertise.

A young person may know considerably more than their parent about a particular online culture, piece of software, game or political controversy. The parent retains authority. The child may possess more domain knowledge.

Schools face the same problem.

The teacher is no longer automatically the person in the room with the greatest access to information. That does not make the teacher unnecessary. It makes the teacher's role harder.

When answers are abundant, helping someone distinguish a plausible answer from a good one becomes more important.

PISA 2022 offers a useful glimpse of this problem. Across OECD countries, 73 per cent of students reported no trouble finding learning resources online by themselves. Yet only 51 per cent said they could easily evaluate the quality of online information. Among lower-performing students, 60 per cent could not easily judge its quality.[^02-oecd]

The scarce resource is shifting.

It was once access to knowledge.

Increasingly, it is **judgment**.

This makes some adult responses to technology feel oddly inadequate.

Taking away the phone may remove a distraction. It does not teach judgment.

Banning ChatGPT may preserve the integrity of this week's homework. It does not prepare a sixteen-year-old to use a system like it responsibly for the next fifty years.

Nor should we romanticise access.

More information is not automatically more freedom.

UNICEF's 2025 review of childhood in a digital world reaches a more complicated conclusion than either technology enthusiasts or technology critics might like. Digital experiences can help children build skills, play and connect. They can also expose children to harmful content and experiences. Connectivity is a prerequisite for developing many digital skills, but access alone is not enough. What young people actually do online and what happens to them there matter enormously.[^02-unicef]

There is another uncomfortable question here: whose values are embedded in the systems doing the explaining?

UNESCO has warned that generative AI can reproduce biases and cultural assumptions contained in training data and has called for age-appropriate, privacy-conscious and human-centred use in education.[^02-unesco]

This matters because childhood has always involved adults deciding when a young person is ready to encounter something.

The internet weakened that gate.

AI may weaken it further.

Perhaps the task therefore changes.

The goal cannot be to restore information scarcity. That world is disappearing.

Nor should the goal be to hand thirteen-year-olds unrestricted access to every system and simply call it empowerment.

The challenge is to help someone develop the capacity to live with enormous access before they possess enormous power.

How do you evaluate what a machine tells you?

How do you decide what deserves your attention?

How do you recognise manipulation?

When should you ask another person?

How do you live with knowledge about problems you cannot personally solve?

How do you distinguish understanding something from merely having received an explanation of it?

These sound like digital-literacy questions.

They are actually questions about growing up.

For previous generations, adults could sometimes protect children by controlling what they knew.

That option is becoming weaker.

We may have to replace it with something much more difficult:

**helping young people know what to do with knowing.**

# 3. When Did Childhood Become a Résumé?

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/when-did-childhood-become-a-resume/`

**Dek:** When every hobby, competition and project can become evidence for a future application, what happens to the parts of childhood that are allowed to go nowhere?

**SEO title:** When Did Childhood Become a Résumé? | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Childhood is increasingly filled with activities that double as future credentials. What happens when exploration slowly becomes optimisation?

**Tags:** youth, education, pressure, childhood, achievement

**Suggested related articles:** [Personalised Everywhere, Standardised at School](/youthful-futures/personalised-everywhere-standardised-at-school/), [More Educated, Less Certain](/youthful-futures/more-educated-less-certain/)

Ask a child what they do outside school, and the answer increasingly sounds like a professional biography.

Football.

Piano.

Coding.

Debate.

Model United Nations.

Robotics.

Dance.

Olympiads.

Volunteering.

Language classes.

Test preparation.

Internships.

Content creation.

None of these things is bad. Many are wonderful. Children should discover things, make things, play sports, perform, compete and become good at something.

The interesting question is when all of it began to feel consequential.

A hobby used to be allowed to be something you did badly because you enjoyed it. Now it can become evidence.

Evidence that you are disciplined.

Interesting.

Well-rounded.

Talented.

A leader.

Somebody a future school, university or employer should select.

The résumé has moved backwards into childhood.

It would be easy to blame ambitious parents.

I think that misses the more interesting story.

Parents respond to systems.

When access to attractive universities, secure jobs or prestigious opportunities feels scarce, families look for whatever advantage is available.

When grades stop distinguishing applicants because everybody applying has high grades, another layer emerges.

Leadership.

Projects.

Sport.

Service.

Competitions.

Portfolios.

When everybody begins doing those, distinction has to be found somewhere else.

The arms race can be perfectly rational for each individual family while being exhausting for everyone collectively.

There are signs of the pressure across different education systems. In PISA 2022, 47.3 per cent of students across OECD countries said they worried that they were not prepared for life after compulsory education. Another 43.6 per cent worried that they would not have enough money to do what they wanted afterwards.[^03-oecd]

The WHO's large Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study provides another window. Across 44 countries and regions in Europe, Central Asia and Canada, school pressure rose between 2018 and 2022. Among fifteen-year-old girls, 63 per cent reported feeling pressured by schoolwork, compared with 54 per cent four years earlier. Among boys the equivalent figure rose from 40 to 43 per cent.[^03-who]

These figures are not a global verdict on childhood.

Academic pressure differs enormously between countries and within them. Poverty can place a child under a different kind of pressure from elite university competition.

Millions of young people are still fighting simply to stay in education. UNICEF reported 272 million children and young people out of school in 2024, including 130 million of upper-secondary age.[^03-unicef]

That contrast is important.

There are children whose futures are constrained because they cannot access enough education, and children whose futures feel constrained by the fear that no amount of education will be enough.

Both are questions of opportunity.

What worries me about the résumé childhood is not that young people work hard.

Difficulty is not inherently harmful, and adults sometimes underestimate how much satisfaction young people get from mastering something difficult.

The problem is when exploration becomes optimisation.

The difference is subtle.

A child learning guitar because something about music grips them may practise harder than a child learning guitar because it will strengthen an application.

Both may eventually become excellent musicians.

But they are learning something different about themselves.

One discovers:

*I care about this.*

The other risks discovering:

*This makes me more valuable.*

That distinction matters especially during adolescence, when identity itself is under construction.

We are asking someone who does not yet know who they are to begin producing evidence of who they will become.

Technology intensifies this because comparison no longer stops at the school gate.

A talented sixteen-year-old used to compare themselves mostly with people they could see.

Now they can encounter the best sixteen-year-old pianist, programmer, footballer, debater, entrepreneur and artist on Earth before breakfast.

The exceptional becomes ordinary through exposure.

We should be careful here too.

Seeing what other young people create can be exhilarating. It can expand a child's sense of possibility. A teenager in a small town can discover peers who care about astrophysics, animation or obscure music in a way that would have been almost impossible a generation ago.

The same mechanism that generates comparison can generate belonging.

That is why "reduce pressure" is too simple a prescription.

Some pressure comes from meaningful ambition.

Some comes from financial insecurity.

Some comes from social comparison.

Some comes from parental fear.

Some comes from young people's own desire to become excellent.

Treating all of it as pathology strips them of agency.

I am more interested in a different question.

**Does childhood still contain enough activity whose value does not depend on where it leads?**

Can you paint without becoming an artist?

Play a sport without making the team?

Learn code without founding something?

Read without improving your vocabulary?

Volunteer without documenting it?

Build something terrible and never upload it?

The strange thing about a résumé is that it gives every experience a destination.

You did this so that someone in the future would conclude something about you.

But some of the most important things young people do may matter precisely because they go nowhere.

They are how you discover what you love, what bores you, what you are bad at, what kind of people you enjoy being around and what you want to spend three hours doing even if nobody rewards you.

A childhood that prepares somebody for everything may accidentally leave too little room for them to discover anything.

And perhaps that is the question worth asking before we add the next class, credential or competition:

**If nobody were ever going to see this on an application, would we still want the child to do it?**

# 4. Personalised Everywhere, Standardised at School

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/personalised-everywhere-standardised-at-school/`

**Dek:** Young people live in systems that constantly adapt to them, until they enter one of the most consequential systems in their lives.

**SEO title:** Personalised Everywhere, Standardised at School | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** AI can personalise explanations instantly, but schools still rely on common pace and common assessments. Which parts of education should be shared and which should adapt?

**Tags:** education, assessment, AI, personalisation, schools

**Suggested related articles:** [Adult Information, Child Power](/youthful-futures/adult-information-child-power/), [AI Arrived Before Apprenticeship](/youthful-futures/ai-arrived-before-apprenticeship/)

A teenager can open Spotify and hear music selected from millions of songs based partly on what they listened to yesterday.

YouTube reconstructs itself around what they watch.

Games continuously adjust difficulty.

Social feeds learn what holds their attention.

An AI tutor can explain the same mathematical idea through football, cooking, diagrams or a conversation and try again when the first explanation fails.

Then the teenager goes to school.

Thirty people sit in a room.

Chapter six begins today.

The examination is on Friday.

Everyone gets forty-five minutes.

There are good reasons schools work this way.

A society needs common knowledge.

Teachers cannot construct thirty entirely different schools inside one classroom.

Shared experiences matter.

Standardised assessments can expose inequalities that would be easy to hide if every learner were evaluated differently.

A common qualification can allow a student from an unknown school to demonstrate competence to a university or employer that has never met them.

Standardisation is not simply evidence that education failed to modernise.

But something around it has changed.

Young people are growing up in environments where personalisation increasingly feels like the normal relationship between a person and a system.

In education, perhaps the most consequential part of their lives, adaptation often stops precisely when the stakes are highest.

The tension is becoming sharper as generative AI enters learning.

A student can now ask for an explanation at exactly the level they understand, request another example, change language, generate practice questions and continue asking without embarrassment.

In principle, that approaches something educators have wanted for centuries: responsive individual support at very low marginal cost.

But technology's history in education should make us cautious.

UNESCO's global review of education technology argues that technology improves some kinds of learning in some contexts, but that benefits can disappear when technology is used excessively or without qualified teachers. Its core recommendation is not to maximise technology, but to decide whether a technology is appropriate, equitable, evidence-based and sustainable for the educational problem at hand.[^04-unesco]

The world is already experiencing the backlash.

By March 2026, UNESCO counted 114 education systems with national restrictions on mobile phones in schools, representing 58 per cent of countries worldwide. That was up from 24 per cent in June 2023.[^04-bans]

There is evidence behind some of this concern.

In PISA 2022, 30 per cent of students across OECD countries reported becoming distracted by digital devices in mathematics lessons.[^04-oecd]

So the future is unlikely to be every child sitting alone with an infinitely personalised machine.

That would solve one problem by creating another.

Education is not merely information entering an individual brain.

It is also the experience of encountering other people.

Waiting while someone else speaks.

Explaining your thinking.

Discovering that another person interpreted the same story differently.

Negotiating disagreement.

Being bored occasionally.

Performing.

Failing publicly.

Helping somebody who understands less than you.

Asking for help from somebody who understands more.

UNESCO makes this tension explicit. Technology can support learning, but should not supplant the human connection on which teaching and learning depend.[^04-unesco]

The harder question is therefore not personalised **or** standardised.

It is deciding what should be which.

Perhaps every learner does need some common intellectual ground.

Perhaps multiplication should not become optional because a child prefers dinosaurs.

Perhaps citizenship requires encountering histories and ideas you did not personally select.

But why must time be standardised?

Why must evidence of understanding always be standardised?

Why does one child have to stop because the lesson is over while another is expected to move on despite not understanding it?

And if two students can demonstrate the same mathematical reasoning through different contexts, how much should the context matter?

AI makes these questions harder to avoid because it exposes how much variation was always possible.

The constraint was partly economic.

One teacher could not continuously generate thirty explanations, thirty practice sets and thirty sequences of feedback.

A machine increasingly can.

That does not mean the machine should decide what learning is.

It means some of our old explanations for why education must look a certain way are becoming weaker.

Assessment may eventually become the most uncomfortable part of this conversation.

We increasingly tell young people that learning can follow their curiosity, pace and interests.

Then, at the moment society decides whether the learning "counts", we often compress everybody back into identical conditions.

There are reasons for that too.

Personalising assessments could introduce bias, make comparison difficult and allow wealthy families to negotiate easier pathways.

Standardisation sometimes protects fairness precisely by refusing to personalise.

But there is a difference between **common standards** and **identical demonstrations**.

We should probably spend more time thinking about it.

The future of education may not be a personalised curriculum for every child.

It may instead be a system with a common intellectual core but far more flexibility in the paths, pace, practice, support and evidence through which students reach it.

That requires something harder than buying software.

It requires answering a philosophical question that technology has allowed us to postpone.

**Which parts of education should belong to the individual, and which should belong to all of us?**

# 5. AI Arrived Before Apprenticeship

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/ai-arrived-before-apprenticeship/`

**Dek:** AI can now perform the junior work through which generations of professionals learned. What happens if we automate the bottom rung of the ladder?

**SEO title:** AI Arrived Before Apprenticeship | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** AI is changing entry-level work before we have redesigned how beginners learn. The risk may not be losing whole professions, but losing the path into them.

**Tags:** AI, work, careers, apprenticeships, future of work

**Suggested related articles:** [The Broken Handoff](/youthful-futures/the-broken-handoff/), [More Educated, Less Certain](/youthful-futures/more-educated-less-certain/)

For a long time, becoming good at something involved doing a lot of work that you would eventually stop doing.

The junior lawyer reviewed documents.

The new analyst cleaned spreadsheets.

The young designer produced rough variations.

The first-year researcher summarised papers.

The apprentice programmer fixed simple bugs.

The intern took notes.

Much of this work was tedious.

It was also how people learned.

Generative AI has arrived with a compelling proposition:

perhaps humans no longer need to do so much tedious work.

That sounds wonderful until we ask a slightly different question.

**What if the tedious work was also the apprenticeship?**

This is one of the least discussed problems in the conversation about AI and jobs.

We spend enormous amounts of time debating whether artificial intelligence will replace professions.

The more immediate problem may be what happens to the bottom rung of those professions.

A 2026 World Economic Forum report produced with PwC estimated that more than one in three young workers globally are in occupations with medium-to-high exposure to AI-driven task change.[^05-wef]

The earliest labour-market evidence deserves caution, particularly because much of it comes from wealthy countries.

But it is beginning to show why entry-level work deserves special attention.

Researchers at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, analysing payroll records covering millions of US workers through June 2026, found no evidence of widespread economy-wide job displacement associated with AI. Yet employment among workers aged 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations stood 19 per cent below where it would have been had it kept pace with their less-exposed peers. Experienced workers in the same occupations did not show the same gap.[^05-stanford]

It is one country and an evolving working paper, not proof of a global future.

But it is precisely the kind of asymmetry worth watching.

This creates a strange possibility.

AI may not eliminate the architect, accountant, programmer, designer or lawyer.

It may instead eliminate enough of the work through which someone **became** one.

There is already a version of this problem in education.

A student who cannot yet write a strong essay can now produce one.

Someone who cannot program can generate functioning code.

Someone who cannot draw can produce an image.

Someone who has never synthesised a complicated body of research can ask a machine to do it.

We normally describe this as cheating when it happens in school and productivity when it happens at work.

That distinction will become harder to maintain.

The more important distinction may be between **using a tool to extend competence** and **using a tool to bypass the formation of competence**.

Experienced professionals often know which parts of an AI-generated output are wrong because they possess the expertise the machine is augmenting.

Beginners may not.

Here is the paradox of powerful tools for beginners.

The people most capable of getting value from them may be people who already learned without them.

None of this is an argument for making young people perform pointless tasks because previous generations had to.

Technology has always removed work.

Calculators changed mathematics.

Search engines changed research.

Computer-aided design changed engineering.

Nobody seriously proposes that young architects should draw every technical plan by hand simply to preserve tradition.

The challenge is identifying what the obsolete labour was secretly teaching.

Perhaps document review taught lawyers to recognise patterns.

Perhaps manually debugging code exposed programmers to how systems fail.

Perhaps writing ten bad drafts gave a writer an internal sense of a sentence.

Perhaps performing basic analyses taught a researcher to notice when numbers did not make sense.

If AI produces the output and removes those encounters, we need another way to produce the learning.

Schools face exactly the same problem.

Employers may have to confront it even faster.

For decades, organisations have complained that graduates arrive without experience while relying on junior positions to create experienced workers.

If entry-level tasks become easily automated, companies could discover that they have optimised away their own talent pipeline.

The answer is probably not protecting every entry-level task.

It is designing better apprenticeships.

A junior employee using AI might produce work faster, then spend more time explaining why the output is correct.

They might compare machine-generated alternatives, diagnose errors, observe experienced colleagues making decisions or take responsibility for increasingly ambiguous problems.

In other words, the unit of learning shifts from **production** toward **judgment**.

This could ultimately produce better early careers.

Nobody needs nostalgia for photocopying documents or formatting PowerPoint slides at midnight.

But it will not happen automatically.

If the economic incentive is simply to replace five junior workers with one experienced worker plus AI, organisations may do exactly that.

The benefits will appear immediately.

The cost may only become visible five years later when there are fewer experienced people to hire.

Young people are entering the workforce at a peculiar moment.

For previous generations, competence generally came before leverage.

Today's beginner can acquire enormous leverage immediately.

We will have to figure out how to preserve the difficult, slow and sometimes frustrating process through which judgment forms without pretending that the tools do not exist.

The question is no longer whether young people should use AI.

They will.

The harder question is:

**How do you learn to do something when a machine can do it before you can?**

# 6. The Broken Handoff

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/the-broken-handoff/`

**Dek:** We spend years preparing young people for the future, then hand them responsibility for translating education into work almost overnight.

**SEO title:** The Broken Handoff from Education to Work | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Schools teach, universities credential and employers demand experience. Young people are left to navigate the gap between education and work.

**Tags:** youth employment, education, careers, skills, transition

**Suggested related articles:** [AI Arrived Before Apprenticeship](/youthful-futures/ai-arrived-before-apprenticeship/), [More Educated, Less Certain](/youthful-futures/more-educated-less-certain/)

There is a moment somewhere between seventeen and twenty-five when an extraordinary institutional transition happens.

For most of childhood, somebody is responsible for your progress.

There is a syllabus.

A timetable.

A teacher.

A parent.

A grade level.

Someone notices when you do not attend.

Someone decides what you are supposed to learn next.

Then, surprisingly quickly, much of that architecture disappears.

You are supposed to find work.

The problem is that no institution clearly owns the handoff.

Schools can say their responsibility is education rather than employment.

Universities can say they provide knowledge, not jobs.

Employers can say applicants should arrive job-ready.

Governments build skills programmes.

Families offer advice based on labour markets they experienced twenty or thirty years earlier.

The young person stands in the middle trying to translate one system into another.

Globally, that transition is already difficult enough to appear clearly in the labour statistics.

About 20 per cent of young people were not in employment, education or training in 2023. Two-thirds of them were women. Youth unemployment stood at 13 per cent, its lowest level in fifteen years, which is genuine progress, but the headline hides enormous regional differences and says little about the security or quality of the jobs young people enter.[^06-ilo]

In low-income countries, unemployment can even be a misleading measure of opportunity because many young people cannot afford to remain unemployed.

They work, but often informally or insecurely.

The ILO estimates that a young adult aged 25 to 29 in a low-income country is at least three times as likely to find only insecure work as someone of the same age in a high-income country. Three in four young workers in sub-Saharan Africa lack secure work.[^06-ilo]

Meanwhile, the education side has expanded.

That is good news.

More young people spend longer in school and training than previous generations did. The ILO estimates that the share of 15-to-24-year-olds in schooling or training rose globally from 38 per cent in 2000 to 48 per cent in 2023.[^06-report]

But expanding one side of the bridge without expanding the other creates its own problem.

The ILO finds substantial educational mismatch in developing economies. Two in three young adult workers there hold qualifications that do not match their jobs well.[^06-ilo]

This is not an argument against education.

It is an argument against assuming that the labour market automatically converts education into opportunity.

The handoff involves things neither side consistently teaches.

How do you understand what a job actually involves before choosing a degree?

How do you demonstrate ability when you have never had a job?

How do you acquire professional norms?

How do you negotiate?

How do you recover after rejection?

How do you distinguish a bad first job from a useful difficult one?

How do you build relationships with adults who are neither teachers nor family?

These things are often described as "soft skills", which has always seemed a strange phrase.

For someone trying to build an adult life, they are rather hard.

The transition is becoming more complicated as jobs themselves change faster.

Employers surveyed for the World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* expected 39 per cent of workers' core skills to change by 2030. AI and big data are among the fastest-growing skills, but so are creative thinking, resilience, flexibility, curiosity and lifelong learning.[^06-wef]

So the destination is moving while young people are crossing the bridge.

The obvious policy response has been "skills".

Teach more coding.

More AI.

More vocational education.

More employability.

Some of that is necessary.

But I wonder whether we have framed the problem too narrowly.

The difficult part of early adulthood is not simply acquiring a list of skills.

It is learning how to navigate a world in which the requirements keep changing.

That suggests the handoff needs to be less like graduation and more like apprenticeship.

Young people need repeated contact with real work before education ends.

Employers need meaningful ways of taking chances on people without prior experience.

Schools need career exploration that goes beyond asking a fourteen-year-old which profession they want.

Universities need to treat the transition into work as part of the educational journey rather than an optional placement-office service.

And employers need to accept something uncomfortable.

An entry-level worker is, by definition, unfinished.

The demand for "job-ready" graduates sometimes sounds like an employer asking the education system to absorb the entire cost of training their workforce.

Young people pay the price when neither side accepts responsibility.

The broken handoff also explains why advice from adults can feel increasingly unsatisfying.

"Study hard."

"Get a degree."

"Learn to code."

"Build your network."

"Use AI."

Each may be sensible.

None is a reliable map.

Maybe what young people need from us is not another promise that following the correct sequence will make everything work.

Maybe they need institutions that stay with them slightly longer while they learn how uncertain the sequence actually is.

We have built enormous systems to prepare young people for the future.

Then, at the exact moment the future begins, we often tell them to figure it out.

[^06-wef]: World Economic Forum, *Future of Jobs Report 2025: Skills Outlook*. https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/3-skills-outlook

# 7. More Educated, Less Certain

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/more-educated-less-certain/`

**Dek:** Education still matters enormously. The problem is that the old promise linking qualification, work and security has become less predictable.

**SEO title:** More Educated, Less Certain | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Young people are spending longer in education while facing a less predictable relationship between qualifications, secure work and adult stability.

**Tags:** education, employment, careers, credentials, economic security

**Suggested related articles:** [The Broken Handoff](/youthful-futures/the-broken-handoff/), [Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable](/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/)

One of the most powerful promises made to young people in the last century was simple.

Get educated and your life will become more secure.

It was never universally true.

Race, class, gender, geography and luck have always shaped what education could deliver.

But as a social bargain, it was powerful enough that families across the world reorganised themselves around it.

Stay in school.

Finish secondary education.

Go to university if you can.

Acquire the credential.

Move upward.

That belief produced enormous progress.

More young people are staying in education.

Professional worlds once accessible to narrow elites have opened to millions more people.

Education continues to matter.

But the bargain feels less predictable.

The ILO's global youth employment research captures the contradiction sharply. In low-income countries in 2023, unemployment among 15-to-29-year-olds with advanced education averaged 21 per cent, compared with 5.8 per cent among those with basic education. In lower-middle-income countries, unemployment among the advanced-education group was nearly double the rate among the basic-education group.[^07-ilo]

This does not mean educated young people are worse off.

The same ILO report finds that, in low- and middle-income countries, tertiary education still brings important advantages in job quality, including lower rates of informal and low-paid work.

The problem is different.

Education has expanded faster than many economies have created the high-skilled jobs graduates expect it to lead to.[^07-ilo]

In richer countries, the anxiety can take another form.

A degree may still offer substantial long-term benefits while no longer guaranteeing the kind of immediate stability students imagined when taking on the time and, in some systems, debt required to obtain it. The ILO also documents rising overeducation in parts of the labour market, where young adults hold qualifications above those typically required for their work.[^07-ilo-figures]

This produces a strange psychological problem.

A person can have made every conventionally responsible choice and still feel behind.

They studied.

They qualified.

They worked.

Yet the house, savings, secure contract or predictable career progression associated with "doing well" can remain distant.

The response is often to tell young people to become even more employable.

Add a credential.

Learn another tool.

Complete a certificate.

Build a personal brand.

Acquire AI skills.

Network.

The résumé grows while certainty does not.

There is an irony here.

The more uncertain the labour market becomes, the more responsibility we place on the individual to keep optimising themselves for it.

That pressure appears in surveys of young workers.

Deloitte's 2026 global Gen Z and Millennial Survey found that only 25 per cent of Gen Z respondents preferred fast-paced career progression defined by rapid promotion. Forty-four per cent preferred steady growth, while 21 per cent were willing to make lateral moves or even take a step back to gain useful experience. At the same time, 76 per cent still expressed interest in senior leadership at some point in their career.[^07-deloitte]

That is hard to describe as a generation becoming less ambitious.

Another interpretation is that the definition of progress is changing under different conditions.

Young people may be asking a perfectly reasonable question.

What exactly are they climbing toward?

The corporate ladder made intuitive sense when employment relationships lasted longer and each rung delivered a relatively comprehensible increase in security, authority and income.

A world of restructurings, contract work, automation, portfolio careers and rapidly changing skill requirements makes the ladder look more like a climbing wall.

Progress can be sideways.

You can move up, discover the skill you built is suddenly less valuable, move across industries, retrain and begin climbing again.

This is not necessarily worse.

Some people gain far more freedom from flexible careers than they would have had inside the rigid organisations of the past.

New industries create opportunities quickly.

Remote work has opened geographical possibilities for some workers.

Young people can build businesses, audiences and professional reputations without waiting for institutional permission.

The mistake is treating new possibility as equivalent to new security.

They are different things.

That distinction also explains why discussions about young people's attitudes toward work can become so moralistic.

Employers complain about loyalty.

Young workers complain about exploitation.

Older workers recall staying in jobs for years.

Younger workers leave.

But loyalty is not simply a personality trait.

It is a relationship between expectations.

If an employer reserves the right to restructure whenever economic conditions change, a young employee may rationally reserve the right to leave whenever a better opportunity appears.

The old bargain was partly relational.

The emerging bargain is increasingly transactional.

Perhaps the real issue is not that young people expect too much from work.

It may be that work is expected to provide too many things because other forms of security have weakened.

Income.

Health insurance in some countries.

Identity.

Friendship.

Purpose.

Learning.

Status.

Community.

A path to housing.

A path to family formation.

No job can reliably carry all of that.

UNFPA's 2026 survey gives us a glimpse of what young adults say they actually want. Financial security was considered important by 81 per cent for entering a partnership and by 88 per cent for parenthood. Stable employment mattered to 87 per cent when considering parenthood.[^07-unfpa]

These are not people rejecting conventional adulthood.

Many are waiting to feel capable of sustaining it.

Perhaps that is why the promise of education needs updating.

Education cannot honestly guarantee a stable destination.

But it can do something more useful than endlessly feeding people into a credential race.

It can give someone enough knowledge to understand the world, enough competence to contribute to it, enough flexibility to change direction and enough confidence to remain a learner after formal schooling ends.

That is a less comforting promise.

It does not say:

"Do this and everything will work out."

It says:

"The world may keep changing. We will help you become someone who can continue changing with it."

For a more uncertain century, that may be the more honest bargain.

# 8. Same Age, Different Amounts of Freedom

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/same-age-different-amounts-of-freedom/`

**Dek:** Age tells us when somebody is supposed to become independent. It tells us much less about the choices they are actually able to make.

**SEO title:** Same Age, Different Amounts of Freedom | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Two young people of the same age can have radically different autonomy because of gender, class, geography, care responsibilities and money.

**Tags:** youth, inequality, gender, autonomy, opportunity

**Suggested related articles:** [Are Boys and Girls Growing Up in Different Worlds?](/youthful-futures/boys-and-girls-growing-up-in-different-worlds/), [Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable](/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/)

Age is one of the simplest ways we organise childhood.

At thirteen you can do this.

At sixteen you can do that.

At eighteen you become legally adult.

It creates the impression that freedom arrives roughly on schedule.

It does not.

Two seventeen-year-olds can have radically different amounts of control over their own lives.

One decides when to go out, whom to meet, what to study and what to wear.

Another asks permission for each of those things.

One has a bedroom, laptop, bank account and parents willing to pay for university.

Another is caring for siblings, contributing to household income or travelling hours to school.

One enters adulthood with savings.

Another enters it with family debt.

And in much of the world, gender changes the equation further.

Globally, girls aged 5 to 14 spend an estimated 160 million more hours every day on unpaid care and domestic work than boys of the same age.[^08-unicef-care] Among 10-to-14-year-olds globally, 16 per cent of girls spend more than 21 hours a week on household chores, compared with 8 per cent of boys. In South Asia and the Middle East and North Africa, girls are three times as likely as boys to cross that threshold.[^08-unicef-adolescent]

That is not merely an inequality in chores.

It is an inequality in time.

Time to study.

Time to play.

Time to make friends.

Time to practise something.

Time to discover what you might be good at.

The divergence continues into work.

Two-thirds of young people globally who are outside employment, education or training are women.[^08-ilo]

Even access to the supposedly borderless internet is shaped by the same old structures.

In 2025, 77 per cent of men globally were online compared with 71 per cent of women, a gap amounting to roughly 280 million more male than female internet users. The divide remains largest in lower-income settings.[^08-itu]

The important word here is not inequality.

It is **freedom**.

We often measure inequality through outcomes.

Who earned more?

Who completed school?

Who became a manager?

Freedom asks an earlier question.

What options were genuinely available?

The distinction matters enormously when talking about young people.

A twenty-year-old who lives with their parents because family life is important to them is in a different situation from somebody who lives there because they cannot afford to leave.

A young woman who chooses not to work outside the home is in a different situation from one prevented from doing so.

A teenager who spends evenings caring for a younger sibling may love doing it and still have fewer free hours than a classmate.

The visible behaviour can be identical.

The freedom behind it is not.

This is why global conversations about "this generation" collapse so quickly.

Even legal adulthood does not bring equal agency.

The World Bank's 2026 *Women, Business and the Law* study examined 190 economies and concluded that no economy provides women with equal economic opportunities across the legal and institutional framework it measures. Only 4 per cent of women live in economies close to full legal equality.[^08-worldbank]

Class changes freedom just as dramatically.

A young person with financial support can take an unpaid internship, leave a harmful job, move cities, prepare for an examination for a year, start a business that fails or choose work they find meaningful.

Someone without that support may appear more "practical".

Often, practicality is simply what choice looks like when the margin for error is small.

Geography matters too.

The internet has expanded access to knowledge but has not erased physical infrastructure.

A course can be online.

A laboratory cannot always be.

A remote job can exist, but reliable electricity and broadband may not.

A young person can learn that an opportunity exists without having any realistic route to reach it.

This is why access can create frustration as well as empowerment.

You can now see the lives that are unavailable to you in extraordinary detail.

It is also why I am wary of stories about extraordinarily resilient young people overcoming impossible odds.

Those stories can be genuinely inspiring.

They can also allow adults to admire someone's resilience rather than ask why so much resilience was required.

The goal should not be to produce young people capable of surviving every obstacle we place in front of them.

It should be to remove obstacles that serve no good purpose.

This does not mean eliminating difficulty.

A meaningful life contains difficulty.

Learning something hard contains difficulty.

Relationships contain difficulty.

Building competence requires frustration.

But there is a difference between **difficulty that develops a person and constraint that simply reduces their options**.

Perhaps this is a better way of thinking about equity among young people.

Not making everyone's life identical.

Not guaranteeing identical outcomes.

But asking whether young people have a reasonable field of possibility from which to construct their lives.

The same age does not mean the same adulthood is available.

Before asking what a young person chose, it may be worth asking:

**What were they actually free to choose?**

# 9. Are Boys and Girls Growing Up in Different Worlds?

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/boys-and-girls-growing-up-in-different-worlds/`

**Dek:** Young men and women are increasingly reporting very different views of gender and equality. The harder question is how they came to experience the same society so differently.

**SEO title:** Are Boys and Girls Growing Up in Different Worlds? | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Global surveys reveal a striking gender gap among young adults on feminism and equality. Are young men and women losing a shared account of reality?

**Tags:** gender, youth, social media, masculinity, feminism

**Suggested related articles:** [Same Age, Different Amounts of Freedom](/youthful-futures/same-age-different-amounts-of-freedom/), [Where Do Young People Go to Belong?](/youthful-futures/where-do-young-people-go-to-belong/)

There is a popular story about younger generations and gender.

Each generation becomes more progressive than the one before it.

History moves forward.

The story is beginning to look messier.

A 2025 study conducted by Ipsos and the Global Institute for Women's Leadership at King's College London surveyed nearly 24,000 people across 30 countries.

On several questions about gender equality, the largest gap between men and women appeared not among older generations but among Gen Z.[^09-kcl]

Fifty-three per cent of Gen Z women surveyed described themselves as feminists, compared with 32 per cent of Gen Z men.

Sixty per cent of Gen Z men agreed that men were being expected to do too much to support equality, compared with 38 per cent of Gen Z women.

Fifty-seven per cent of young men said efforts to promote women's equality had gone so far that they discriminated against men, compared with 36 per cent of young women.[^09-kcl]

The following year's 29-country Ipsos research found a similar divide on the last of those questions: 57 per cent of Gen Z men agreed that women's equality had gone so far as to discriminate against men, compared with 38 per cent of Gen Z women.[^09-ipsos]

These findings do not tell us that young men around the world are becoming one thing or young women another.

The national differences are enormous.

"Gen Z" once again hides more than it reveals.

But the divergence itself deserves attention.

How can people of roughly the same age, attending the same universities and working in the same organisations come to experience gender relations so differently?

The internet is an obvious suspect.

It may also be too convenient an answer.

Young men and women can spend time in very different digital environments.

Platforms learn from tiny differences in behaviour and gradually build increasingly different feeds.

A young man who pauses on one video about male status can be offered another.

A young woman engaging with content about misogyny can receive an expanding stream of examples documenting it.

Neither feed needs to contain false information to construct a distorted sense of the whole.

If I show one person one hundred examples of women being mistreated by men and another one hundred examples of men being humiliated or dismissed by women, both may reasonably believe they are learning about society.

They are.

They are also learning from different samples of it.

We should be careful about jumping from that mechanism to causal claims.

The evidence does not justify saying algorithms created the gender divide.

Economic changes, family structures, political movements, educational differences and the changing roles of women and men all matter.

Online cultures can reflect divisions that already exist just as easily as they intensify them.

But digital environments are unusually good at organising a feeling into a worldview.

That matters during adolescence.

A young man who feels lonely, romantically unsuccessful or economically insecure can encounter an explanation saying the system has become hostile to men.

A young woman experiencing harassment or restrictions can encounter an explanation saying patriarchal structures shape much of her life.

Parts of both explanations may be true.

The danger comes when an explanation becomes total.

At the same time, offline life remains unequal.

Girls continue to perform substantially more unpaid domestic work globally. Young women make up two-thirds of young people outside education, employment and training. Women still face legal, economic and safety barriers across much of the world.[^09-structural]

This makes the conversation particularly difficult.

A young woman may look at measurable structural inequality and find male resentment absurd.

A young man may experience educational difficulty, loneliness or economic insecurity personally and find abstract claims about male privilege equally absurd.

Each may believe the other is refusing to recognise reality.

Perhaps the mistake is assuming there can only be one legitimate form of disadvantage at a time.

Advances for women do not require pretending every young man is thriving.

Concern for boys does not require rewriting the persistent inequality girls face.

The more interesting question is what happens if young men and women cease to see one another as potential collaborators in building a fairer world and increasingly experience the other group as the reason their own lives are difficult.

There are already signs of heightened perception of conflict. In the 2025 study, 59 per cent of Gen Z respondents across 30 countries said there was tension between men and women in their society, compared with 40 per cent of Baby Boomers.[^09-kcl]

Maybe the challenge facing this generation is therefore not simply gender equality.

It is **shared reality**.

Can two people enter a conversation about gender without first having to invalidate the other's experience?

Can boys discuss loneliness without blaming women?

Can girls discuss sexism without being asked to reassure boys?

Can schools create spaces where these conversations happen before an algorithm supplies the explanation?

The internet promised to expose everyone to more perspectives.

It may also be creating environments in which people encounter enormous amounts of evidence for the perspective they already inhabit.

Young men and women have never had easier access to one another.

It would be a strange outcome if they ended up understanding one another less.

# 10. Where Do Young People Go to Belong?

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/where-do-young-people-go-to-belong/`

**Dek:** We have many places where young people are expected to perform. We may have fewer places where they can simply be.

**SEO title:** Where Do Young People Go to Belong? | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Young people report high levels of loneliness, but blaming phones misses the physical spaces and repeated relationships through which belonging is built.

**Tags:** loneliness, belonging, youth, cities, community

**Suggested related articles:** [Are Boys and Girls Growing Up in Different Worlds?](/youthful-futures/boys-and-girls-growing-up-in-different-worlds/), [Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable](/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/)

There are many places where young people are expected to perform.

School.

University.

Work.

Sport.

The internet.

There are fewer places where they are allowed simply to be.

This may be one reason the global conversation about loneliness among young people feels both surprising and unsurprising.

WHO's recent global work on social connection estimates that about one in six people experience loneliness. Adolescents report the highest prevalence of any age group, at around 21 per cent. The rate is higher in lower-income countries, which is a useful reminder that loneliness is not merely a wealthy-country side effect of smartphones.[^10-who]

The World Happiness Report offers another striking measure.

In 2023, 19 per cent of young adults globally said they had nobody they could count on for social support, a 39 per cent increase from 2006.[^10-whr]

Yet there are enormous national differences.

In a 22-country dataset used in the report, more than 30 per cent of young adults in Japan said they had nobody they felt close to, while the figure was below 10 per cent in Nigeria, Egypt and the Philippines. Mexico, India and Egypt were among the countries where the quality of social support among young adults improved over time.[^10-whr]

There is no single global loneliness story.

And that matters because the easiest explanation is usually the phone.

Phones clearly change social life.

They can interrupt conversation and turn every quiet moment into an opportunity to leave the room mentally.

Social media can produce comparison, harassment and the peculiar experience of watching people socialise without you.

But the same device allows a queer teenager in a hostile environment to find people like them, a migrant to remain part of a family thousands of kilometres away and someone with an obscure interest to discover a community that simply does not exist locally.

Connection and disconnection can arrive through the same screen.

Perhaps we should look at the physical world too.

Where exactly is a sixteen-year-old supposed to spend three unstructured hours with friends?

In many cities, space is expensive.

Cafés expect purchases.

Malls are commercial.

Streets may be unsafe or hostile to pedestrians.

Parks are unevenly distributed.

Schools close.

Homes may be too small or heavily supervised.

A teenager hanging around without a purpose is often treated as a problem to be managed.

Then we wonder why teenagers spend so much time online.

Adults have workplaces, restaurants and homes they control.

Younger people have far fewer spaces where they have legitimate authority.

The sociologist Ray Oldenburg used the term "third places" for spaces that are neither home nor work where ordinary social life happens.

Whatever terminology we use, young people need their own version.

A basketball court.

A library.

A tea shop.

A park bench.

A youth club.

A gaming café.

A religious community.

A street corner where nobody moves them on.

A friend's house where parents are nearby but not constantly present.

These spaces can look trivial from the outside.

They are where friendship acquires repetition.

That matters because belonging rarely emerges through one spectacular interaction.

It develops through seeing someone again and again without needing a reason.

Modern institutions sometimes make that harder.

University students may commute rather than live near campus.

Young workers may switch employers frequently.

Remote work can remove casual encounters.

High housing costs push people farther from friends or keep them in households where entertaining others is difficult.

Migration creates opportunity while breaking existing networks.

None of these changes is inherently bad.

But together they can reduce what we might call **social infrastructure**.

The World Happiness Report finds that social connection is strongly associated with young adults' well-being. It also describes something hopeful: people can underestimate how willing others are to connect with them, and interventions that correct those beliefs can increase social interaction and well-being.[^10-whr]

That suggests loneliness is not always evidence that people do not want relationships.

Sometimes people want relationships while assuming other people do not.

This is where the internet produces another odd contradiction.

Young people can know what hundreds of people are doing while being uncertain whom they can call.

The distinction between **contact** and **support** becomes important.

A person can receive twenty messages and have nobody they trust with bad news.

They can have thousands of followers and nobody to eat dinner with.

They can also have deep friendships maintained primarily through screens.

Counting interactions tells us surprisingly little.

Maybe the useful question is not how many hours young people spend online.

It is whether their lives contain relationships in which they are known, needed and expected to return.

This has implications far beyond mental health services.

If loneliness is partly an infrastructure problem, therapists cannot solve it alone.

Schools can design for belonging.

Universities can make friendship formation an explicit part of orientation rather than an accidental by-product.

Employers can understand that early-career workers are not only acquiring tasks but entering adult communities.

Cities can build places where young people can exist without continually spending money.

Families can recognise that unsupervised social life is not wasted time.

And perhaps adults can stop treating every apparently idle gathering of teenagers as suspicious.

We spend a lot of energy asking how to keep young people away from harmful communities online.

The more interesting challenge may be giving them enough good communities to belong to elsewhere.

Because telling someone to spend less time online does not answer the question waiting underneath.

**Where should they go instead?**

# 11. Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/`

**Dek:** Young adults have more possible lives to choose from, while the economic conditions beneath traditional milestones have become less predictable.

**SEO title:** Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** Work, housing, marriage and parenthood no longer arrive in one reliable sequence. More freedom has expanded adulthood while making it harder to know whether you are behind.

**Tags:** adulthood, family, housing, work, young adults

**Suggested related articles:** [More Educated, Less Certain](/youthful-futures/more-educated-less-certain/), [Young People Are Not Failing Adulthood. Adulthood Changed.](/youthful-futures/adulthood-changed/)

For a long time, adulthood came with a sequence.

Finish education.

Find work.

Leave home.

Marry.

Buy a house.

Have children.

The sequence was never universal.

It was shaped by particular middle-class experiences and often relied on women's unpaid labour.

In many societies, multigenerational households have always been normal.

Millions of people never followed the sequence at all.

But as an idea, it was powerful.

It told people roughly where they were supposed to be.

The timetable is becoming less reliable.

Not because young people have collectively rejected adulthood.

Recent global evidence suggests almost the opposite.

UNFPA's 2026 Demographic Futures Survey heard from more than 108,000 internet-connected adults aged 18 to 39 across 73 countries.

More than two-thirds wanted to marry or live with a partner.

Two children remained the most commonly reported ideal family size across five of seven regional groupings.

What had changed were the conditions people felt they needed before doing those things.

Financial security was important to 81 per cent of respondents considering partnership and 88 per cent considering parenthood.

Stable employment mattered to 87 per cent for becoming a parent.

Emotional readiness mattered to 85 per cent.

Economic and housing conditions were the most commonly reported barriers to forming partnerships.[^11-unfpa]

This complicates a familiar story about young adults.

They are delaying marriage.

They are delaying children.

They are living with parents.

They are unwilling to commit.

Sometimes that may be true.

But delay can also be the behaviour of somebody who takes commitment seriously.

If you believe parenthood requires stable income, housing, emotional readiness and a reliable partner, then uncertainty in any one of those areas can postpone everything downstream.

UNFPA's 2025 *State of World Population* report reached a similar conclusion through a separate survey across 14 countries. Thirty-nine per cent of respondents said financial limitations had affected or would affect their ability to have the family size they desired. Nearly one in five said fears about the future, including climate change, environmental degradation, war and pandemics, had led or would lead them to have fewer children than they wanted.[^11-swp]

The central finding was not that people had stopped wanting families.

Many were struggling to build the families they wanted under the conditions available to them.

This matters because public debate often confuses demographic outcomes with personal preferences.

A falling birth rate becomes evidence that young people no longer value family.

A twenty-eight-year-old living with parents becomes evidence of delayed maturity.

A later marriage becomes fear of commitment.

The explanation may occasionally be cultural.

It may also be rent.

Europe provides a useful illustration of why we need local context.

In 2025, the average age of leaving the parental home across the European Union was 26.3. But it ranged from 21.4 in Finland and 21.8 in Denmark to 31.5 in Croatia, 30.9 in Greece and Slovakia, and 30.2 in Spain and Italy.[^11-eurostat]

Those differences are shaped by labour markets, housing systems, family culture and welfare institutions.

There is clearly no biological age at which a human becomes ready to move out.

Perhaps adulthood has always been more structural than we admitted.

A stable job allows a person to make a long-term housing decision.

Housing makes partnership easier.

Partnership can make parenthood easier.

Childcare affects whether both adults can work.

Work affects financial security.

The milestones are connected.

When one moves, the others move.

The psychological consequence is interesting.

A timetable, even an unfair one, provides certainty.

When the sequence dissolves, freedom expands.

You can change careers at thirty.

Live alone.

Not marry.

Have children later.

Never have children.

Return to education.

Move countries.

Build a company.

Live with friends.

This is enormously valuable.

But freedom carries a quieter burden.

If there is no correct path, every path becomes partly your responsibility.

At twenty-seven you can look at one friend buying a house, another travelling for a year, another raising a child, another completing a doctorate and another earning far more money than you.

All are plausible versions of adulthood.

You cannot live all of them.

Social media makes these parallel lives unusually visible.

The result is not simply envy.

It is something closer to constant counterfactual thinking.

*Should I be doing that instead?*

The phrase "behind in life" becomes strange once there is no single course.

Behind whom?

On which route?

By what schedule?

This may be one of the defining psychological adjustments of young adulthood now.

Previous generations often fought for the freedom to escape rigid social scripts.

Younger generations inherit more of that freedom and must learn how to live without the certainty the script provided.

The answer cannot be to reconstruct one compulsory timetable.

It may be to build stronger conditions beneath many possible ones.

Affordable housing.

Secure work.

Childcare.

Education that remains accessible beyond twenty-two.

Healthcare that does not disappear with a job.

Communities where unmarried adults remain socially included.

Workplaces where parenthood does not permanently derail a career.

The point is not to make people marry earlier, move out earlier or have more children.

It is to make those choices more genuinely theirs.

Perhaps adulthood was never the sequence itself.

Perhaps adulthood is the increasing ability to make meaningful commitments and carry responsibility for them.

If that is true, the right question for a twenty-eight-year-old is not:

**Why haven't you reached the next milestone?**

It is:

**Do you have enough security and freedom to build the life you actually want?**

[^11-swp]: UNFPA, *State of World Population 2025: The Real Fertility Crisis*. https://www.unfpa.org/swp2025

# 12. Young People Are Not Failing Adulthood. Adulthood Changed.

**Slug:** `/youthful-futures/adulthood-changed/`

**Dek:** Many behaviours described as generational flaws make more sense when we look at the systems young people are adapting to.

**SEO title:** Young People Are Not Failing Adulthood. Adulthood Changed. | Youthful Futures

**Meta description:** The final Youthful Futures essay asks whether we have mistaken adaptations to new economic, technological and social conditions for flaws in young people.

**Tags:** youth, adulthood, institutions, future, generational change

**Suggested related articles:** [13 to 30 Is Not a Generation](/youthful-futures/13-to-30-is-not-a-generation/), [Adulthood Has Lost Its Timetable](/youthful-futures/adulthood-has-lost-its-timetable/)

There is a recurring genre of conversation about young people.

They cannot concentrate.

They are anxious.

They do not want to work hard.

They change jobs too often.

They live with their parents.

They are always online.

They use AI instead of thinking.

They do not date properly.

They are delaying marriage.

They are having fewer children.

They are simultaneously too political and politically disengaged.

Every generation eventually complains about the next one.

That fact alone does not mean every complaint is wrong.

Some things really are changing in worrying ways.

One in seven adolescents globally experiences a mental disorder. Suicide is the third leading cause of death among people aged 15 to 29.[^12-who-mental]

Adolescents report the highest rates of loneliness of any age group in the WHO's latest global estimates.[^12-who-social]

School pressure has risen in many countries.

A fifth of young people globally remain outside employment, education or training.[^12-ilo]

AI appears capable of disrupting precisely the entry-level tasks through which some workers acquire experience.[^12-ai]

There are things worth worrying about.

But after looking across the issues in this series, I keep returning to a different pattern.

Many behaviours we interpret as changes in young people are also reasonable responses to changes in the world around them.

Young workers change jobs in labour markets where employers restructure frequently.

Young adults postpone parenthood when housing, childcare and stable employment feel uncertain.

Teenagers retreat into digital spaces partly because digital spaces offer communities, entertainment and autonomy that their physical environments do not.

Students use AI because the most powerful learning and production tool they have encountered is available instantly while schools are still working out what to do with it.

Young people accumulate credentials because the promise attached to each individual credential has weakened.

They worry about the future because the future is genuinely difficult to predict.

This does not make every response healthy.

Something can be understandable and still be harmful.

A teenager can rationally retreat online because they are lonely and become lonelier there.

A worker can rationally keep switching jobs and never build deep professional relationships.

A student can rationally use AI to maximise grades and accidentally avoid learning.

A parent can rationally add more activities to a child's schedule and collectively contribute to a culture in which no child feels they are doing enough.

Rational individual behaviour can produce terrible collective outcomes.

That is precisely why blaming young people gets us nowhere.

The task is to understand the system producing the behaviour.

Three large changes sit underneath much of this series.

The first is **abundance**.

Young people have access to astonishing quantities of information, entertainment, advice, communities and potential pathways.

For hundreds of millions, a phone contains more learning resources than an elite university library held a generation ago.

But abundance creates new scarcities.

Attention.

Judgment.

Trust.

Time.

A sense of enough.

The second is **optionality**.

More young people can imagine lives outside the path prescribed by their family, community or geography.

That is one of the great achievements of modernity.

But possibility has expanded faster than certainty.

The freedom to choose a life also creates the burden of wondering whether you chose correctly.

The third is **institutional lag**.

Technology, labour markets, family formation and culture are changing quickly.

Schools, universities, employment systems, regulation and cities generally move more slowly.

UNICEF's work on the future of childhood identifies demographic change, environmental and climate crises, and frontier technologies as three megatrends that will reshape children's lives through the coming decades.[^12-unicef]

These are not distant possibilities.

Children are already living inside them.

The mismatch may be the defining problem.

We have an education system largely designed around a world where information was scarce, while young people live in one where answers are abundant.

We tell young people to acquire experience while AI begins automating beginner work.

We tell them to become financially independent while housing can be difficult to access in many economic centres.

We celebrate individual choice while leaving people to privately solve childcare, mental health, career transitions and loneliness.

Then we observe their adaptations and diagnose a generational problem.

Maybe the institutions deserve some diagnosis too.

The good news is that young people do not appear to have abandoned the future.

UNFPA's 2026 survey is valuable here.

Across a geographically diverse sample of more than 108,000 internet-connected young adults, two-thirds remained somewhat or very positive about the future. More than two-thirds wanted to marry or live with a partner. Financial security, stable work and emotional readiness mattered enormously to decisions about parenthood.[^12-unfpa]

This does not sound like a generation that wants nothing.

It sounds like a generation asking whether the structures around them can support the things they want.

The question should therefore not be whether young people are resilient enough for the future.

I increasingly dislike the way we use that word.

Resilience easily becomes a way for institutions to avoid changing.

If housing is unaffordable, become resilient.

If work is insecure, become adaptable.

If technology destroys your profession, reskill.

If school causes anxiety, practise mindfulness.

If your community disappears, build a support network.

There is value in every one of those individual capacities.

But eventually a society has to ask whether the environment itself deserves adjustment.

That does not absolve young people of responsibility.

Growing up necessarily involves acquiring responsibility.

Learning to focus even when distraction is available.

Learning to tolerate discomfort.

Learning to do difficult things before you are good at them.

Learning to disagree without treating disagreement as harm.

Learning that not every institution will adapt itself perfectly around you.

Learning that freedom does not mean freedom from obligation.

The mistake is framing responsibility as a choice between the individual and the system.

Young people need to become capable of living in the world.

Adults need to build a world worth becoming capable of living in.

Those projects belong together.

This is why the series is called **Youthful Futures**, rather than a series about Gen Z.

The plural matters.

There is no single future arriving for young people.

A child growing up in Lagos, Seoul, Bengaluru, Helsinki, São Paulo, Gaza or rural Montana is not moving toward the same adulthood.

Climate, demography, artificial intelligence, politics, migration and economics will distribute opportunity and insecurity very differently.

Nor are young people merely waiting for those futures to happen to them.

They will make them.

The thirteen-year-old using AI to finish homework today will eventually be a teacher, parent, engineer, artist, voter, founder, bureaucrat or politician deciding what the technology should do.

The sixteen-year-old arguing about gender online may eventually raise a son or daughter.

The twenty-two-year-old struggling to enter the labour market may eventually decide how their own organisation hires beginners.

The twenty-eight-year-old wondering whether they can afford a child may eventually shape the policies another generation of parents inherits.

Youth is temporary.

The conditions encountered during it are not.

They become institutions, habits, expectations and eventually culture.

So perhaps the central question running through all twelve pieces is not whether today's young people are prepared for the future.

It is something that places more responsibility on the rest of us too.

**What futures are we asking them to prepare for, and have we built the institutions that make those futures possible?**
